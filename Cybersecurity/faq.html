Cybersecurity FAQ

20 honest answers to the questions beginners ask most.

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computers, phones, networks, and data from theft, damage, or unauthorized access. Think of it as digital locks, alarms, and security guards for your online life.
Yes — when done with written permission. Ethical hackers are hired to test systems and report weaknesses so they can be fixed. Hacking without permission is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide, even with good intentions.
Start with basics: how computers and networks work, then learn about common attacks and defenses. Practice on free legal labs like TryHackMe or HackTheBox, and follow structured guides like the ones on this site.
No. Many professionals are self-taught. Employers care about skills, certifications, and practical lab experience far more than degrees.
CompTIA Security+ is the best beginner certification — widely recognized and vendor-neutral. After that, consider eJPT or CEH for ethical hacking, or OSCP when you are ready for advanced hands-on testing.
Earnings vary by country and role. Entry-level analysts often earn a solid salary, and experienced penetration testers and security engineers are among the highest-paid IT professionals. Bug bounty hunters can also earn significant extra income legally.
With consistent daily study (2-3 hours), most beginners reach entry-level readiness in 6-12 months. It depends on your background and effort — there are no shortcuts, but the path is clear.
Basic scripting (Python is ideal) helps a lot, especially for automation and understanding attacks. You do not need to be a software developer, but you should be comfortable reading code and writing simple scripts.
Ethical hacking is the broad field of authorized security testing. Penetration testing is a specific service within it — a structured, time-boxed simulated attack on a system, delivered with a professional report.
Companies like Google and Microsoft pay ethical hackers who find and responsibly report security flaws in their systems. It is 100% legal, done through platforms like HackerOne and Bugcrowd, with clear scope rules.
Check the sender's real email address, hover over links before clicking, never enter passwords from email links, and enable two-factor authentication. When in doubt, contact the company directly through its official website.
Length matters most: 14+ characters with a mix of uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and symbols. Better yet, use a password manager to generate and store unique passwords — then you only remember one master password.
Public WiFi can be risky — attackers on the same network may snoop on unencrypted traffic. Use a reputable VPN, avoid banking on public WiFi, and make sure sites use HTTPS. Better: use your phone's mobile data.
Ransomware encrypts your files and demands payment. Avoid it by: keeping offline backups, updating software promptly, not opening suspicious attachments, and using reputable antivirus. Never pay the ransom — it funds crime and rarely works.
A VPN encrypts your traffic, which helps on public WiFi and hides browsing from your ISP. Choose a reputable paid VPN with a no-logs policy. Note: a VPN does not make you anonymous and does not protect against phishing or malware.
2FA requires a second proof of identity beyond your password — usually a code from an app like Google Authenticator. It blocks the vast majority of account takeovers. Use app-based codes, not SMS, when possible.
Yes. Phones are hacked through malicious apps, phishing links, outdated software, and sometimes zero-click exploits. Protect yours: update promptly, install apps only from official stores, and be skeptical of unexpected links.
Stay calm: 1) Disconnect from the internet. 2) Change passwords from a clean device, starting with email. 3) Enable 2FA everywhere. 4) Scan with reputable antivirus. 5) Check bank statements. 6) Report to your bank and local cybercrime helpline if money is involved.
