🛰️ VPN Guide

No hype, no sponsored rankings — just honest education about what a VPN does, what it cannot do, and how to choose one if you need it.

⚠️ Ethical Use Only: These tools are for defensive purposes — securing your own accounts and systems. Never use security knowledge against systems you do not own or lack written permission to test.

🔍 What a VPN actually does

  • Creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and the VPN company's server. Your internet provider and local WiFi snoopers see only encrypted traffic to that server.
  • Hides your IP address from the websites you visit — they see the VPN server's IP instead.
  • Can make it appear as if you're browsing from another country.

🚫 What a VPN does NOT do

  • Not antivirus: it won't stop malware, phishing, or malicious downloads.
  • Not anonymity: websites still track you via cookies, logins, and browser fingerprinting. You're shifting trust from your ISP to the VPN company.
  • Not a license to break rules: it doesn't make illegal activity legal.
  • Not magic speed: routing through another server usually slows your connection slightly.

⚠️ About "free" VPNs

Many free VPNs have been caught logging and selling browsing data, injecting ads, or having weak security. A free VPN can be worse than no VPN. If you need one, a reputable paid provider with an audited no-logs policy is the honest recommendation. For casual use, your browser's HTTPS and your home network are already decent protection.

About This Tool

What this tool does

The VPN Comparison Guide explains what virtual private networks actually do — and what they do not do — and helps you evaluate providers honestly. A VPN encrypts your internet traffic between your device and the VPN server and hides your real IP address from the sites you visit, which is genuinely useful on untrusted networks.

The guide compares providers on the criteria that matter: jurisdiction and logging policy, independent security audits, encryption standards, kill-switch functionality, speed impact, and pricing transparency. It also corrects common myths, because VPN marketing routinely promises more than the technology delivers.

It is for travelers, remote workers, journalists, and anyone using public Wi-Fi. A VPN is one defensive tool among many — not a invisibility cloak — and this guide helps you choose one for the right reasons.

How to use it

  1. Read the "what a VPN does and does not do" section first, so your expectations are realistic.
  2. Decide your use case: public Wi-Fi protection, travel, remote work, or bypassing censorship all weight the criteria differently.
  3. Compare providers on logging policy, independent audits, jurisdiction, and kill-switch support — not on advertising claims.
  4. Prefer providers with a clear no-logs policy backed by independent audits and real-world tests, not just marketing copy.
  5. After subscribing, enable the kill switch, test for DNS and IP leaks, and keep the app updated.
  6. Remember the VPN does not replace other defenses: you still need updates, 2FA, and phishing awareness.

Defensive security tips

  • Use a VPN on untrusted networks. Hotel, airport, and café Wi-Fi are exactly where encrypted tunneling earns its keep.
  • Always enable the kill switch. It blocks all traffic if the VPN drops, preventing accidental exposure of your real IP address.
  • Be wary of free VPNs. Running VPN servers costs money; free providers often pay for it with ads, data collection, or selling bandwidth. If privacy is the goal, a free VPN can be worse than none.
  • A VPN does not make you anonymous. Logged-in accounts, browser fingerprinting, and cookies still identify you. It protects the connection, not your identity.
  • Check for leaks. Periodically verify with an independent leak test that your real IP and DNS requests are not escaping the tunnel.

FAQs

Does a VPN protect me from hackers?

Partially. It protects your traffic from snoopers on the local network and your ISP, but it does nothing against phishing, malware you download yourself, or data you hand to websites voluntarily.

Can my VPN provider see my activity?

Technically, yes — your traffic passes through their servers. That is why a trustworthy no-logs policy, verified by independent audit, is the most important criterion when choosing.

Do I need a VPN at home?

Less so. Your home network and ISP relationship are relatively stable, and HTTPS already encrypts most web traffic. A VPN matters most on networks you do not control.

Will a VPN slow down my connection?

Somewhat — encryption and rerouting add overhead. Modern protocols and nearby servers keep the impact small for browsing and streaming, though competitive gaming may still feel it.
