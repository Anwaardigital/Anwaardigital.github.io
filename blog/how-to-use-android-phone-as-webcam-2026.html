How to Use Your Android Phone as a Webcam for Video Calls (2026 Guide)

Most laptop webcams are terrible. They are small, dim, and make you look washed out on every Zoom, Teams and Google Meet call. Meanwhile the camera in your pocket — your Android phone — is a far better camera than anything built into a laptop. It has a bigger sensor, a sharper lens, better low-light performance and autofocus that actually works. The good news is that you can use that phone camera as your computer's webcam, and on many modern Android phones you can do it with no extra app at all. This guide shows you every method — the built-in Android option, the Samsung route, and the free apps for older phones — with wired and wireless setup steps, plus the lighting, framing and sound tips that make the difference between looking professional and looking like a blurry silhouette.

Why Your Phone Beats Your Laptop Webcam

Phone makers spend a huge amount of effort on cameras, because the camera sells the phone. Laptop makers treat the webcam as an afterthought. That gap shows up on every call. A typical laptop webcam records at 720p with a tiny sensor that struggles in anything but bright daylight. A mid-range Android phone from the last few years records at 1080p or higher, handles dim rooms far better, and keeps your face in focus when you lean forward or sit back.

There are other advantages too. Your phone's front camera often has a wider field of view, so you can sit comfortably instead of hunching over the laptop. Many phones let you switch to the rear camera for even better quality. And you can position the phone anywhere — on a tripod, a stack of books, or a cheap phone stand — at eye level, which is the single most flattering camera angle there is. Buying a separate external webcam costs money; your phone is already in your pocket, and for most people it produces a better picture than a webcam in the same price range.

Tip: Before you spend money on a webcam, try your phone for one week of calls. Most people never go back — the difference in picture quality is visible to everyone on the call, not just to you.

What You Need Before You Start

You do not need much, but having the right pieces ready saves frustration halfway through setup.

  • An Android phone running Android 9 or newer for app-based methods. For the built-in USB webcam mode, you need Android 14 or newer. Check your version under Settings → About phone → Android version.
  • A computer running Windows 10, Windows 11, macOS or Linux. The built-in Android webcam mode works with all of them, because the computer sees the phone as a standard USB video device.
  • A USB cable that carries data, not just power. The thin cable that came with a cheap charger sometimes only charges. Use the cable that came with your phone, or any good-quality USB-C cable, and plug it into a USB port on the computer itself rather than a hub for the first test.
  • A way to hold the phone at eye level: a small tripod, a phone stand, or a stable stack of books. A phone leaning against a mug at chest height films up your nose — nobody wants that angle.
  • The same Wi-Fi network on phone and computer, only if you choose the wireless method. Wired USB setup does not need Wi-Fi at all.

One more thing to check before you begin: close any app on the phone that is already using the camera, and make sure the phone is charged or plugged in. Video streaming drains the battery faster than normal use, and a phone that dies mid-meeting is worse than a bad webcam.

Method 1: Android 14+ Built-in Webcam Mode Over USB

Since Android 14, many phones have a built-in webcam mode. No app to install, no account to create, no extra software on the computer. You plug the phone in with a USB cable, tell the phone to act as a webcam, and your video-call app sees it as an ordinary camera. If your phone has this feature, it is the method to use first.

Step-by-step setup

  1. Connect your phone to the computer with a USB data cable.
  2. On the phone, swipe down to open notifications. You should see a USB notification, often saying "Charging this device via USB" or similar. Tap it.
  3. A list of USB options appears: File transfer, USB tethering, MIDI, and — on supported phones — Webcam. Select Webcam.
  4. The phone screen switches to a simple webcam preview screen. It usually shows a preview of your camera, a button to switch between front and rear cameras, and a zoom control.
  5. On the computer, open your video-call app — Zoom, Microsoft Teams, Google Meet in the browser, or any other app — and open its camera settings. Choose your phone from the camera list. It usually appears with your phone's model name, such as "Pixel 8" or "Galaxy A54".
  6. Start a test call or use the app's video preview to check the picture. Adjust the phone's position so your eyes are about one-third down from the top of the frame.

If you do not see a Webcam option in the USB menu, your phone either runs an older Android version or the manufacturer has not included the feature. Samsung phones, for example, do not show this option — use Method 2 instead. Some phones also hide the option until developer options are enabled, but on most Android 14 and 15 phones from Google, Motorola, OnePlus and others it appears directly in the USB menu.

✓ Result: The computer treats your phone exactly like a plug-and-play webcam. No drivers to install on Windows or macOS, and the connection is rock solid because it runs over a cable.

Method 2: Samsung Phones and Link to Windows

Samsung Galaxy phones do not include Android's built-in USB webcam mode, but Samsung and Microsoft built a different route: Link to Windows. With the Link to Windows app on your phone and the Phone Link app on a Windows PC, your Galaxy phone's camera can be used as the webcam for Windows video calls. This is a genuine Samsung-to-Windows feature, not a third-party workaround.

Setting it up

  1. On your Samsung phone, open Settings → Connected devices → Link to Windows and sign in with your Microsoft account.
  2. On your Windows 10 or 11 PC, open the Phone Link app (it comes preinstalled on Windows 11) and sign in with the same Microsoft account.
  3. Pair the two devices by following the on-screen prompts and approving the connection on the phone.
  4. On the PC, open Settings → Bluetooth & devices → Mobile devices, select your phone, and turn on the option to use it as a connected camera.
  5. Open your video-call app and select your Samsung phone in the camera settings. The phone will show a camera preview while it is in use.

The wireless connection is convenient because there is no cable to manage, and the phone can sit on a stand anywhere near the computer. The picture quality is generally very good, though it depends on your Wi-Fi signal. If the video stutters, move closer to the router, or switch to a 5 GHz Wi-Fi band if your router offers one. Note that Link to Windows is a Samsung and Microsoft feature — it is designed for Windows PCs, and it needs both devices signed in to the same Microsoft account.

Method 3: Free Apps for Older Phones — DroidCam, Iriun and Camo

If your phone does not have the built-in webcam mode and is not a Samsung, a free webcam app fills the gap. These apps work on older Android versions and on phones from brands that removed the built-in option. The three best-known options are DroidCam, Iriun Webcam and Camo. All three follow the same idea: you install the app on your phone, install a small companion program on your computer, and the two talk to each other over USB or Wi-Fi. The phone becomes a camera your computer can select in any video-call app.

  • DroidCam: The oldest and most widely used of the three. The free version works over USB and Wi-Fi, supports both front and rear cameras, and includes a microphone option. It is available for Windows and Linux, and the setup is straightforward: install the phone app from the Play Store, install the desktop client from the developer's official website, then connect using the address shown in the phone app.
  • Iriun Webcam: A simpler alternative with a clean interface. It works over USB and Wi-Fi on Windows and macOS, supports up to 4K video on supported phones, and needs very little configuration — the phone app finds the computer automatically when both are on the same network.
  • Camo: The most polished of the three, aimed at people who want fine control over the picture — framing, zoom, focus and exposure adjustments from the computer. It works on Windows and macOS, and the free version covers the basics for everyday calls, with extra features in the paid version.

A word of caution that applies to all three: download the phone app only from the Google Play Store, and the desktop program only from the developer's own official website. Webcam software asks for camera and microphone access, so it is exactly the kind of software that fake download sites imitate. If a site offers a "cracked" or "free pro" version of one of these apps, stay away from it. For general guidance on telling safe downloads from dangerous ones, see our APK safety guide.

Warning: Only grant camera and microphone permission to a webcam app you installed from the Play Store and trust. If an app asks for contacts, location or SMS access to work as a webcam, that is a red flag — a webcam app has no reason to need those permissions.

Wired vs Wi-Fi: Which Connection Should You Use?

Every method above offers a choice between a USB cable and Wi-Fi. The right choice depends on how you call.

  • USB cable — best for important calls. A wired connection is stable, has almost no delay, and charges the phone while you talk. Use it for job interviews, online classes, exams and any meeting where a frozen frame would be embarrassing. The only downside is the cable itself, which limits where the phone can sit.
  • Wi-Fi — best for convenience. Wireless setup is tidy, with no cable on the desk, and the phone can sit further from the computer. Use it for casual calls with family and friends. The picture depends on your Wi-Fi signal, so it can stutter if the router is far away or the network is busy.
  • A practical rule: if the call matters, plug the cable in. If the call is casual, Wi-Fi is fine. Many people keep the phone on a stand with the cable permanently attached at their desk, so switching between the two is never a decision — they just pick the phone up and go.

One more consideration: Wi-Fi mode sends video over your local network, so both devices must be on the same network. If your computer is on a wired office network and your phone is on the office guest Wi-Fi, they may not be able to see each other. In offices and universities, USB is usually the reliable choice.

Using Your Phone Camera in Zoom, Meet, Teams and WhatsApp

Once the phone is connected — by USB, Link to Windows or an app — the rest is the same in every video-call program. The computer sees the phone as just another camera, so you pick it in the app's settings.

  • Zoom: Click the small arrow next to the video icon, or go to Settings → Video, and select your phone under Camera. Zoom's test video preview lets you check framing before joining the meeting.
  • Google Meet: In the meeting, click the three-dot menu, then Settings → Video, and choose your phone. In the browser, Meet may ask permission to use the camera the first time — allow it.
  • Microsoft Teams: Go to Settings → Devices and select your phone as the camera. If the phone does not appear, restart Teams after connecting the phone — Teams sometimes only scans for cameras when it starts.
  • WhatsApp and other apps on the computer: WhatsApp Desktop and WhatsApp Web support video calls on the computer, and both let you choose the camera in their settings. The same applies to Skype, OBS Studio for recording or streaming, and most other video apps — if the app has a camera setting, your phone will appear there.

A practical habit: before every important call, join two minutes early and check the preview. Confirm the picture is your phone camera and not the laptop's built-in one — apps often default back to the laptop camera after an update, and you do not want to discover that halfway through the meeting.

Lighting, Framing and Sound: Looking and Sounding Your Best

A good camera with bad lighting still looks bad. These five adjustments cost nothing and make the biggest difference of all.

1. Light your face, not your back

Sit facing a window or a lamp. Light should fall on your face from the front. A window or bright lamp behind you turns you into a dark silhouette, no matter how good the camera is. If you call in the evening, put a desk lamp behind the computer, aimed at you — a simple white bulb works fine.

2. Put the camera at eye level

Raise the phone so the lens is level with your eyes, or very slightly above. Looking down at a phone on the desk films up your nose and makes you look tired; looking slightly up at a camera on top of a monitor is far more natural. A stack of books is a perfectly good phone stand.

3. Frame yourself properly

Your head and shoulders should fill most of the frame, with a little space above your head. Sit about an arm's length from the phone. Check the background before the call: a tidy wall or bookshelf is fine, an unmade bed or a busy room behind you is distracting. Most video apps also offer a blur-background option if you need it.

4. Use the rear camera when quality matters most

Your phone's rear camera is better than its front camera — bigger sensor, better lens, often optical stabilisation. For a job interview or a recorded lesson, switch to the rear camera and use the computer screen to see yourself. It takes an extra minute to frame the shot, and the picture is noticeably sharper.

5. Fix the sound too

Bad audio ruins a call faster than bad video. The phone's built-in microphone is decent at close range, but if you sit far from it, your voice will sound distant and echoey. Wired earphones with a built-in microphone are the cheapest big upgrade — they cost very little and make your voice clear while keeping room noise out. If you use the phone's microphone, sit in a quiet room and close the door; even a ceiling fan can be loud on a sensitive phone mic. Keep the phone near you, not across the room.

Tip: Record a 30-second test video of yourself before an important call and play it back. You will spot bad lighting, a messy background or echoey audio in seconds — things you would never notice while looking at the preview.

Fixing Lag, No Sound and "Phone Not Detected" Problems

Most problems with a phone-as-webcam setup have simple causes. Work through this list before reinstalling anything.

  • Video lags or freezes: Switch from Wi-Fi to a USB cable first — this fixes most lag immediately. If you must use Wi-Fi, move closer to the router, close other apps using the network, and lower the video resolution in the webcam app settings. A 720p stream that runs smoothly looks far better than a 1080p stream that freezes every few seconds.
  • The computer does not see the phone: Check the cable — a charge-only cable cannot carry video. Try a different USB port, and check the phone's USB notification: it must be set to Webcam (Method 1) or File Transfer, depending on the method you are using. If the phone charges but no USB menu appears, the cable is almost certainly charge-only.
  • No sound, or the wrong microphone: In the video-call app, check the microphone setting separately from the camera setting. Some apps select the phone camera but keep the laptop microphone, or the other way round. Choose the microphone deliberately — phone mic or earphones — and test it with the app's audio test feature.
  • The picture is sideways or upside down: This usually happens when the phone rotates while the app is running. Hold the phone upright, in portrait orientation, and restart the video in the app. If your webcam app has a rotation setting, use it to lock the orientation.
  • The phone gets hot: Video streaming is heavy work, and the phone will warm up. Remove the phone case during long calls, keep the phone out of direct sunlight, and keep the screen brightness low — the webcam preview screen does not need full brightness. If the phone shows a temperature warning, end the call and let it cool.
  • The battery drains fast: Use the USB cable whenever possible, since it charges while you stream. On Wi-Fi, plug the phone into a charger and keep the screen brightness down. Close background apps before the call so the processor is not doing extra work.
  • Another app is using the camera: Only one app can use the phone camera at a time. If the webcam app says the camera is busy, close the camera app, WhatsApp, or any other app that might hold the camera, then try again.

Privacy: Switch the Camera Off When You Are Done

A phone camera pointed at your room is a privacy matter, so treat it like one. When the call ends, disconnect the phone: unplug the USB cable, close the webcam app, or turn off the Link to Windows camera option. Do not leave the phone mounted on its stand, camera facing the room, with a webcam app running in the background. If your webcam app has a "stop" or "disconnect" button, use it — closing the video-call app on the computer is not always enough to stop the phone streaming.

Also check what the webcam app can access. A webcam app needs the camera and the microphone; it does not need your contacts, your location or your files. Review the app's permissions under Settings → Apps and remove anything unnecessary. And remember the same caution that applies to any camera app: never install a webcam app from outside the Play Store, and never install a "modded" version that promises paid features for free. For more on keeping your phone safe, see our guide on checking if your Android phone is hacked and locking apps on Android.

Warning: If a video-call app or webcam app ever shows your camera as active when you are not on a call, close it immediately and check which app holds the camera permission. Android shows a green dot in the top-right corner whenever the camera or microphone is in use — if you see it and you are not on a call, something is wrong.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use my Android phone as a webcam without installing any app?

Yes, if your phone runs Android 14 or newer and includes the built-in webcam mode — just connect with a USB cable and choose Webcam from the USB menu. Samsung phones can use Link to Windows instead, which also needs no third-party app. Older phones need a free app such as DroidCam or Iriun.

Does using my phone as a webcam use mobile data?

No. Over USB, no data is used at all. Over Wi-Fi, the video travels only across your home network between the phone and the computer — it does not go through the internet or use your mobile data, as long as both devices are on the same Wi-Fi network.

Will it work with Zoom, Teams and Google Meet?

Yes. All major video-call apps let you choose your camera, and your phone appears in that list like any other webcam. The same applies to Skype, WhatsApp Desktop, OBS Studio and most recording software.

Is the phone camera really better than a laptop webcam?

For most people, yes — noticeably. Phone cameras have larger sensors, better lenses, autofocus and far better low-light performance than the small 720p cameras built into most laptops. The difference is easy to see in a side-by-side test.

Can I use the rear camera instead of the front camera?

Yes. The built-in Android webcam mode has a button to switch between front and rear cameras, and apps such as DroidCam and Camo offer the same option. The rear camera gives a sharper picture, but you will need the computer screen or a mirror to frame yourself, since you cannot see the phone's screen from behind it.

Does the phone battery drain quickly while streaming?

Yes, video streaming uses more battery than normal use. Over USB this is not a problem, because the cable charges the phone at the same time. Over Wi-Fi, plug the phone into a charger for calls longer than half an hour.

Can I use my phone as a webcam and charge it at the same time?

Yes — that is one of the best reasons to use the USB method. The same cable that carries the video also charges the phone, so the battery stays full for the whole call.

The Bottom Line

Your Android phone is very likely the best webcam you already own. If your phone runs Android 14 or newer, the built-in USB webcam mode takes about two minutes to set up and needs no extra software. Samsung owners can use Link to Windows, and everyone else can use a free app like DroidCam, Iriun or Camo. Use a cable for important calls, Wi-Fi for casual ones, light your face from the front, raise the phone to eye level, and use earphones for clear sound. Do that, and the next time someone on a call says "your video looks really clear", you will know exactly why — and it did not cost you anything.
