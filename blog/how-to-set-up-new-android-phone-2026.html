How to Set Up Your New Android Phone: A Complete First-Time Guide (2026)

A new Android phone is exciting, but the first hour with it decides how well it will serve you for the next two or three years. Rush through the setup screens, tap "Skip" on everything, and you end up with a phone that has no backup, a weak lock screen, missing contacts, and apps asking for permissions you never meant to give. Do it properly — in the right order — and the whole thing takes about thirty minutes, after which your new phone feels like yours: your photos, your chats, your apps, your security, all in place. This guide walks through every step in the order that makes sense, so nothing gets missed. Whether you are switching from an old Android phone, moving from an iPhone, or setting up your very first smartphone, follow these steps and you will finish with a phone that is secure, backed up, and ready to use.

Before you start: what you need ready

Five minutes of preparation saves you from stopping halfway through the setup to hunt for a password. Before you switch the new phone on, gather these:

  • Your old phone, charged and unlocked. You will need it for the data transfer, and it must stay on and nearby during the copy. Charge it to at least 50% first — a transfer that dies halfway through is a headache.
  • Your Google account email and password. This is the account your contacts, photos and app purchases are tied to. If you use two-step verification, keep the second device or backup codes nearby so you can approve the sign-in. If you have forgotten the password, recover it on a computer before you start.
  • Your Wi-Fi network name and password. The setup will download updates and your apps, and that is far faster — and cheaper — on Wi-Fi than on mobile data. If you do not know the Wi-Fi password, check the sticker on the router or ask whoever set it up.
  • Your SIM card and the small ejector pin that came in the box. If your phone uses an eSIM instead of a physical SIM, have the QR code or activation details from your mobile carrier ready.
  • A backup of your old phone. If your old phone is backed up to Google, the transfer will restore your apps and settings automatically. If it is not backed up, turn on backup on the old phone now and let it finish before you begin. Our Android backup guide shows exactly how.
Tip: Do the setup at home on Wi-Fi, not in the shop or on the road. You will download hundreds of megabytes of updates and apps, and a stable connection makes the difference between a smooth half-hour and a frustrating afternoon.

Step 1: Charge, insert the SIM, and power on

New phones usually arrive with some charge, but plug it in for a few minutes anyway — you do not want it dying during the first update. While it charges, put in the SIM card: use the ejector pin to open the SIM tray on the side of the phone, place the SIM in the tray with the gold contacts facing down and the cut corner matching the tray shape, and slide it back in gently. If you force it, stop — it is probably the wrong way round.

If your phone uses an eSIM, there is no tray to fill. You will scan your carrier's QR code during the setup screens, or enter the details manually in Settings → Network & internet → SIMs → Add eSIM. Our eSIM setup guide covers the details if your carrier gave you a QR code.

Now press and hold the power button until the logo appears. The phone will ask you to choose a language and a region — pick the language you are comfortable reading, because every menu after this will use it.

Step 2: Connect to Wi-Fi and sign in with Google

The setup wizard will show a list of nearby Wi-Fi networks. Tap yours, enter the password, and wait for it to connect. If the network does not appear, move closer to the router, or tap "Add network" and type the name manually. Avoid using public or hotel Wi-Fi for the initial setup — you will be signing into your Google account, and a private home network is safer.

Next, sign in with your Google account — the same one you used on your old phone. This single sign-in is what makes everything else work: your contacts sync, your purchased apps reappear, your Google Photos backup resumes, and your calendar and email come with you. Type the email and password carefully. If two-step verification is on, Google will ask you to approve the sign-in on your old phone or enter a code — this is normal and takes a few seconds. If you are setting up a phone for a child or an older parent, use their own Google account, not yours, so their data stays separate.

Warning: Never sign in with your Google account on a phone you do not own, and never let a shop assistant sign in with their own account "just to test it". A Google account tied to a phone controls its backup, its location, and its purchases. If someone else set it up for you, ask them to sign out and sign in with your own account before you leave.

Step 3: Transfer your data from your old phone

When the setup asks "Copy apps and data", tap "Next" and choose your old phone as the source. Android will offer two ways to connect: with a cable (USB-C to USB-C, or with the adapter in the box) or wirelessly over Wi-Fi. A cable is faster and more reliable, especially if you have a lot of photos and videos — use it if you can. If you only have one cable, connect the two phones and follow the prompts on both screens.

You will be asked what to copy. As a rule, copy everything: apps, contacts, messages, photos, videos, call history and device settings. The transfer can take anywhere from five minutes to over an hour depending on how much data you have — photos and videos are the slowest part. Keep both phones close together, plugged into chargers, and do not use either phone until the transfer finishes. If the transfer asks about WhatsApp, it will move your chats too, but WhatsApp needs its own restore step afterwards (see Step 7).

Coming from an iPhone? The same screen works: connect the iPhone with a cable, sign in to your Google account, and Android will copy your photos, contacts, messages and most apps. Some things do not transfer — iMessage chats and most iPhone-only app data stay behind — but your photos, contacts and calendar come across cleanly. Our full data transfer guide covers the iPhone case step by step, including what to do if the cable method fails.

Step 4: Set up your screen lock, fingerprint and face unlock

Do not skip this step. The setup will ask you to choose a screen lock — pick a strong PIN of at least six digits, or a pattern you would not guess from watching someone draw it once. A four-digit PIN like a birth year is the first thing anyone tries. Then, if your phone has a fingerprint sensor or face unlock, set those up too. They are conveniences that sit on top of your PIN: you unlock with your finger or face day to day, and the PIN is the fallback that actually protects the phone.

Register the same finger twice if the phone allows it, and register a second finger — your other thumb or index finger — so a wet or injured finger does not lock you out. With face unlock, set it up in normal indoor light, and look straight at the phone as instructed. Remember that a PIN is still required after a restart, so do not set a PIN you will forget in a week. Write it down somewhere safe at home — not in a note on the phone itself — until you have memorised it.

Step 5: Turn on Find My Device and Google backup

These two settings are the safety net for everything else, and they take less than a minute to confirm. Open Settings → Security → Find My Device (on some phones it is under Settings → Google → Find My Device) and make sure it is switched on. This is the feature that lets you locate, ring, lock or erase the phone from a computer if it is ever lost — but it only works if it was switched on before the phone went missing. Check that location is turned on too, because Find My Device needs it to show where the phone is.

Then go to Settings → Google → Backup and confirm that "Back up to Google Drive" (or "Backup by Google One") is on. Tap "Back up now" and wait for the first backup to complete. This backs up your apps, call history, contacts, device settings and messages, so a lost or broken phone never means losing everything. Photos are backed up separately — open Google Photos, tap your profile picture, and check that backup is on there too. If anything ever happens to this phone, our lost or stolen phone guide explains exactly what to do, but it only works if these settings are on first.

Step 6: Install your essential apps

If you transferred your data, your old apps should already be reinstalling from the Play Store — check the Play Store app to see the download progress. If you set the phone up as new, install your essentials now, and install them only from the Play Store. The apps most people need on day one are: a messaging app (WhatsApp), a browser (Chrome is pre-installed), a maps app, your banking apps, an email app, and any work or school apps. Install the banking apps one at a time and sign in carefully — they will usually ask for an SMS code or a call to verify the new device.

Resist the urge to install everything at once. A fresh phone is the best time to leave behind the apps you never used. Open the app drawer from your old phone mentally — the game you played twice, the shopping app you opened once — and skip those. Fewer apps means less clutter, fewer notifications, and better battery life. If your new phone came with pre-installed apps you do not want, you can uninstall most of them, or disable the ones that cannot be uninstalled. Our bloatware removal guide shows how to do this safely without rooting the phone.

Step 7: Set up WhatsApp and your other accounts

WhatsApp is the app most people care about most, so give it its own careful step. Open WhatsApp, agree to the terms, and verify your phone number with the SMS code it sends. If you transferred your data with a cable, WhatsApp may offer to restore your chats from the transfer directly. If not, it will ask to restore from a Google Drive backup — tap Restore and wait. Do not skip the restore: once you start using WhatsApp without restoring, the old backup can be overwritten and your chat history may be lost. Our WhatsApp backup and restore guide walks through this in detail, including what to do if the restore button does not appear.

Then sign in to your other accounts: email, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, your bank, and any school or work apps. Do this one app at a time and use your password manager or your saved passwords rather than resetting every password on the spot. If you use two-factor authentication — and you should — each sign-in may ask for a code from your authenticator app or an SMS. That is a good sign: it means your accounts are protected. If you have not set up two-factor authentication yet, our two-factor setup guide explains it in plain steps.

Step 8: Tame notifications and app permissions

A new phone is quiet for about a day, and then every app starts asking for your attention. Spend five minutes now and you will save yourself months of buzzing. Go to Settings → Notifications and turn off notifications for apps that do not need to interrupt you — games, shopping apps, and news apps can all wait until you open them. Keep notifications on for messaging, calls, email and banking. Then open each app you installed and, when it asks for permissions, think before you tap Allow. A maps app genuinely needs your location; a torch app does not. A messaging app needs your contacts; a photo editor does not need your call logs. Our app permissions guide explains what each permission really means, and our notification management guide shows how to silence the noisy ones without missing the important ones.

Step 9: Personalise it — wallpaper, home screen and widgets

Now the fun part. Long-press an empty spot on the home screen and tap Wallpaper to pick a background — your own photo works well, or one of the phone's built-in wallpapers. Then arrange your home screen: put the four or five apps you use every day on the first screen, move the rest into the app drawer, and remove any widget you did not ask for. A clean first screen is not just prettier — it means you find what you need in one glance instead of hunting through pages.

Add one or two widgets you will actually use: a clock with the date, a weather widget, or a calendar widget showing today's appointments. If you use your phone a lot at night, turn on Dark mode in Settings → Display — it is easier on the eyes and can save a little battery on phones with OLED screens. If your phone offers gesture navigation, try it for a day; many people prefer it to the three-button bar once they get used to it, and you can always switch back in Settings → System → Gestures.

Step 10: Update the software and check the battery settings

Before you call the setup finished, go to Settings → System → Software update and install any update it offers. New phones often ship with software that is a few months old, and the first update usually includes security fixes and bug fixes worth having. The update may take ten minutes and one restart — let it finish on Wi-Fi, plugged into the charger.

While you are in Settings, check the battery. Go to Settings → Battery and look at Battery Saver and Adaptive Battery — leave Adaptive Battery on, because it learns which apps you actually use and stops the others draining power in the background. If your phone offers a "protect battery" or "charge to 85%" option, turning it on can help the battery last longer over the life of the phone, especially if you charge overnight. Our battery life guide covers the rest of the settings that are worth changing once you have used the phone for a week and know which apps are the hungriest.

Tip: Restart the phone once after all the updates and app installs are done. A fresh restart clears the temporary files from the setup process and lets the phone start clean with everything in place.

Your first-day checklist

Run through this list before you put the old phone away. If you can tick every box, the setup is genuinely done:

  • SIM or eSIM working — you can make a call and receive an SMS
  • Wi-Fi connected and mobile data working
  • Signed in to your own Google account
  • Contacts, photos and messages transferred from the old phone
  • Screen lock set (PIN plus fingerprint or face unlock)
  • Find My Device switched on
  • Google backup switched on, first backup completed
  • WhatsApp restored with your chat history
  • Banking apps signed in and verified
  • Software update installed
  • Notifications and permissions reviewed
  • Old phone signed out of banking and payment apps before you sell it or give it away

That last point matters: do not factory-reset or hand over your old phone until you have checked everything above on the new one. Open WhatsApp on the new phone and confirm your recent chats are there. Open your gallery and check your photos. Send a test message to a family member. Only when you are sure the new phone has everything should you erase the old one — and when you do, remove your Google account from it first, then use the factory reset in Settings, so the next owner cannot get back into your account.

Troubleshooting common setup problems

  • "Could not sign in" to Google: check the password on another device first. If it is right and sign-in still fails, the usual cause is two-step verification — approve the prompt on your old phone, or use a backup code. If you recently changed the password, wait a few minutes and try again; Google sometimes blocks sign-ins for a short time after a password change.
  • Transfer stuck or very slow: keep both phones close together and plugged in, and stay on the same Wi-Fi network. If a wireless transfer stalls, cancel it and retry with a cable — the cable method is almost always faster for large photo libraries. Do not start using either phone during the transfer.
  • Apps not reinstalling after transfer: open the Play Store, tap your profile picture → Manage apps & device, and check the download queue. Paused or failed downloads can be resumed from there. If an app says "not compatible with this device", it may need an update from the developer, or it may genuinely not support your new phone's Android version.
  • WhatsApp restore button missing: the backup may be under a different Google account, or the backup may never have been made. Check on the old phone: WhatsApp → Settings → Chats → Chat backup, and confirm which Google account and date it shows. Then make sure the new phone is signed in to that same account before you open WhatsApp.
  • No mobile signal after inserting the SIM: restart the phone first. If there is still no signal, check that the SIM is seated properly in the tray, and confirm with your carrier that the SIM is active. If you moved from a phone with an eSIM, the physical SIM may need to be activated by the carrier before it works.
  • Phone feels hot during setup: this is normal for the first hour. Downloading updates, restoring apps and indexing photos all at once makes the processor work hard. Leave it on a table (not under a pillow or in a pocket) while it finishes, and it will cool down once the setup is complete.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to set up a new Android phone?

About 30 minutes of active steps, plus the time the data transfer takes — usually 10 to 60 minutes depending on how many photos and videos you have. Budget an hour in total, on Wi-Fi, with both phones charged.

Should I set up my new phone as new, or copy from my old phone?

Copy from your old phone in almost every case — it brings your contacts, messages, photos, apps and settings across in one step. Set up as new only if your old phone is broken, you are starting completely fresh, or the old phone had problems you do not want to carry over, such as malware or a full storage.

Do I need a Google account to use an Android phone?

Practically, yes. You can use an Android phone without signing in, but you will not be able to download apps from the Play Store, back up your data to Google, or use Find My Device. Sign in with your own account during setup — it is what makes the phone work properly.

Will my WhatsApp chats move to the new phone?

Yes, if you copy your data during setup or restore from a Google Drive backup. The key is to restore before you start chatting on the new phone. If you skip the restore, the old backup can be overwritten, so always restore first.

What should I do with my old phone after the transfer?

Keep it for a week as a spare while you confirm everything moved across. Then, before selling it or giving it away, sign out of your Google account, remove any payment cards, and factory-reset it from Settings → System → Reset options → Erase all data. That wipes your personal data so the next owner starts clean.

Setting up a new Android phone properly is not complicated — it is just a sequence of small steps, and the order matters. Get the SIM and Wi-Fi right, sign in with your own Google account, transfer your data, lock the phone down with a PIN and fingerprint, and switch on Find My Device and backup before anything else. The rest — apps, wallpaper, widgets — is the easy and enjoyable part. Do it once, do it properly, and your new phone will be secure, backed up, and ready for the next few years.
