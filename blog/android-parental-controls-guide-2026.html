Android Parental Controls: The Complete Family Safety Guide (2026)

Handing your child their first phone is a milestone — and a worry. Between inappropriate content, endless screen time, surprise app purchases, and strangers in game chats, an unprotected phone is a genuine risk for a young child. The good news: Android has the most complete built-in parental controls of any mobile platform, and they are free. Google Family Link plus a handful of settings in the Play Store, Chrome, and YouTube can turn any Android phone into a genuinely kid-safe device. This guide walks through the full setup for children of every age, the settings most parents miss, and the honest limits no filter can fix.

In this guide:
  • Start with Google Family Link: setup and supervision levels
  • Screen time: daily limits, bedtime, and per-app timers
  • App approvals and Play Store content filters
  • Web and YouTube filtering: Chrome SafeSearch, YouTube Kids, Restricted Mode
  • Location sharing and finding a lost device
  • Adjusting controls as your child grows (and the teen years)
  • What parental controls cannot do — the conversation that matters more

Step 1: Set Up Google Family Link

Family Link is Google's official parental supervision system, and everything else in this guide builds on it. You will need two things: your own Google account (as the parent) and a Google account for your child. If your child is under 13 (or the applicable age in your country), Google requires parental consent to create the account — which is exactly what Family Link walks you through.

Install the Google Family Link app on your own phone and sign in. Tap to add your child and follow the setup: it either creates a new supervised Google account or links an existing one. Then sign in to that child account on the child's device. From that moment, the child's device is supervised — you manage apps, screen time, filters, and location from your own phone, and the child sees a Family Link section in their Settings explaining what is being managed.

💡 Set expectations before you set up. Tell your child what Family Link does and why, in age-appropriate words: "This helps me keep you safe, and as you show responsibility, the rules get looser." Children who understand the system cooperate with it; children who discover it feel spied on — and a determined teenager can bypass almost any filter they resent.

Step 2: Set Screen Time Limits and Bedtime

In Family Link, tap your child, then Controls → Screen time. You can set a daily limit for weekdays and weekends separately — a common starting point is 2 hours on school days and 3–4 on weekends, adjusted for homework needs. When the limit is reached, the device locks with a friendly screen; the child can request bonus time, which you approve or decline from your phone.

Just as important is Bedtime (in the same screen): pick a time when the phone locks every night — say 9:30 PM — and a morning unlock time. Phones in bedrooms at night are one of the biggest drivers of lost sleep in children, and this setting solves it without nightly arguments. You can also lock the device instantly any time with the Lock now button — dinner time, homework time, family time.

Per-app time limits

Some apps deserve their own limits: an hour of a game is fine, four hours is not. In Family Link go to Controls → App limits, pick an app, and set its daily cap. This is more effective than a single global limit, because it lets educational apps and reading stay unlimited while games and social video get boundaries.

Step 3: Control Which Apps Get Installed

Every app your child tries to install from the Play Store can require your approval first. In Family Link: Controls → Google Play → Require approval for, and choose All content. Your child sends an approval request from their phone; you get a notification and can approve or decline, even seeing the app's age rating and permission list first.

Also set Play Store content filters in the same section: Apps & games → rating limit (choose the age bracket matching your child, e.g. "Everyone" or "Everyone 10+"), and do the same for Movies if they watch on the device. This blocks age-inappropriate apps and films from even appearing in search results — not just from installing.

⚠️ Approval is only as strong as your attention. It is tempting to approve requests on autopilot. Take ten seconds to check the app's rating, reviews, and why it needs its permissions. A flashlight app asking for contacts and location is a red flag at any age — approve the request only when the app genuinely needs what it asks for.

Step 4: Filter the Web and YouTube

Filtering the browser and YouTube is where many parents stop short — and where children encounter the most inappropriate content.

Chrome SafeSearch

In Family Link go to Controls → Filters on Google Chrome → SafeSearch and set it to filter explicit results. For younger children, also enable "Try to block mature sites", which attempts to block adult websites entirely in Chrome. It is not perfect, but it removes the vast majority of accidental exposure.

YouTube settings by age

For children under about 9, the right answer is YouTube Kids — a separate app with curated, age-banded content. For older children on the main YouTube app, turn on Restricted Mode (in the YouTube app: profile → Settings → General → Restricted Mode) and in Family Link restrict the YouTube experience level under content restrictions. Restricted Mode hides most mature videos and comments, though determined kids know it is imperfect — which is why app time limits matter alongside it.

Step 5: Location Sharing and Finding the Phone

Family Link includes location tracking: Location → See your child's location shows where the device is on a map. This is genuinely useful for younger children — knowing they arrived at school safely, or finding a lost phone under the sofa. For teenagers, agree on when you will actually check it; location tracking that is used for constant surveillance damages trust, while tracking used for safety ("text me when you arrive" replaced by a glance at the map) is usually accepted.

Also make sure Find My Device is enabled on the child's phone (Settings → Security → Find My Device) so a lost phone can be located, locked, or erased from any browser.

Adjusting Controls as Your Child Grows

Parental controls should loosen over time — the goal is a young adult who manages their own digital life, not a teenager who counts the days until they can uninstall everything. A practical progression:

  • Ages 6–9: Maximum supervision. YouTube Kids only, strict app approvals, short screen time, bedtime lock, blocked mature sites, location on. The phone lives in shared family spaces.
  • Ages 10–12: Main YouTube with Restricted Mode, app approvals for new installs but freedom within approved apps, per-app game limits, SafeSearch on. Start discussing why each rule exists.
  • Ages 13–15: Loosen app approvals (keep Play Store rating filters), extend screen time, keep bedtime but negotiate it. Shift from monitoring to mentoring — review their app list together monthly instead of approving each install.
  • Ages 16+: Google actually ends Family Link supervision at 18, and children 13+ can technically choose to stop supervision (with you being notified). Plan for this: by 16, the controls should be mostly advisory, with the relationship — not the software — doing the real work.

What Parental Controls Cannot Do

No filter is complete, and knowing the gaps keeps you honest:

  • They don't filter everything. Filters miss content, especially in other languages, in live streams, and in comments. Assume your child will eventually see something inappropriate, and make sure they know they can tell you without getting in trouble.
  • They don't stop other people's devices. A friend's unfiltered phone at a sleepover bypasses every setting you configured. Digital safety education travels with your child; device settings don't.
  • They don't replace the conversation. Research consistently finds that children who can talk openly with parents about online experiences handle problems — bullying, stranger contact, disturbing content — far better than children who are only filtered. The most important parental control is a child who tells you when something feels wrong.
  • They don't teach judgment. Controls are training wheels. Every year, consciously hand over one more piece of responsibility and talk through the decisions, so that by 18 your child has years of guided practice — not a sudden cliff edge.
💡 The 10-minute monthly review. Once a month, sit with your child and look at their screen time report and app list together. Celebrate the healthy stuff ("you read for an hour a day — great"), discuss the concerning stuff without lecturing, and adjust one rule together. This single habit does more for long-term safety than any filter setting.

Set up Family Link this weekend, layer on the Play Store filters and SafeSearch, lock in a bedtime, and agree on the rules out loud. The technology takes about 30 minutes to configure; the conversation takes the rest of childhood — and it is the part that actually works.
