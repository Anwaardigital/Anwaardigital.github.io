How to Use Offline Maps on Your Android Phone (2026 Guide)

You are driving through a mountain road, the signal bars disappear, and your navigation freezes at the worst possible moment. Or you land in a new city, your mobile data has not started working yet, and you cannot even find your hotel. Both problems have the same simple fix, and it takes five minutes before you leave home: download the map in advance. Your Android phone can store whole cities on its own storage and guide you with GPS alone — no mobile data, no WiFi, no signal required. This guide shows you how to set it up properly, what offline maps can and cannot do, and how to keep them fresh.

Why Offline Maps Are Worth Setting Up

Navigation apps normally fetch map tiles, traffic and route data from the internet as you move. That works fine in a city with strong coverage, but it fails exactly where you need navigation most: rural roads, mountains, tunnels, border areas and foreign countries where roaming is expensive or unavailable. A downloaded map removes that dependency. Your phone's GPS receiver works directly with satellites, so it knows your position even with no SIM card and no data connection at all.

Offline maps also save you money and battery. Without a constant data connection, the app stops fetching tiles over the network, which reduces both mobile data usage and the radio activity that drains your battery on long drives. And because the map is already on your phone, it loads instantly — no waiting for tiles to appear while you are already moving.

The one thing to understand up front: offline maps are a safety net, not a full replacement for online navigation. They give you roads, places and turn-by-turn directions, but not live traffic, road closures reported in the last few minutes, or public transport schedules. Plan around those limits and they will never let you down.

Step 1: Download a Map Area Before You Travel

Do this at home on WiFi, the night before you travel. Downloading over mobile data works too, but a city map can be hundreds of megabytes, and WiFi is faster and free.

  1. Open Google Maps and make sure you are signed in to your Google account.
  2. Search for the city or region you are visiting — for example, the city centre of your destination.
  3. Tap the place name at the bottom of the screen to open its details, then tap the three-dot menu (or the download icon) and choose Download offline map.
  4. A rectangle appears over the map. Pinch and zoom to adjust it so it covers everywhere you expect to go — your hotel, the places you plan to visit, and the roads between them. The app shows the download size as you adjust the area.
  5. Tap Download and wait for it to finish. Keep the app open or let it run in the background on WiFi.

You can also download from your profile: tap your profile picture, then Offline maps, then Select your own map. This is the same screen where you manage everything later, so it is worth remembering. For a longer trip, download several areas — your home city, each destination city, and the corridor between them — rather than one giant rectangle. Smaller downloads finish faster and are easier to update.

💡 Tip: Download the map for your own city too, not just holiday destinations. It is the map you will need if a storm knocks out local networks, and it costs you nothing to keep.

Step 2: Navigate Offline with GPS

Using a downloaded map is delightfully boring: it just works. Open Google Maps, search for a place inside your downloaded area, and tap Directions exactly as you would online. The app detects that you have no connection (or that the area is downloaded) and routes using the stored map. Turn-by-turn voice guidance works the same way, and your blue dot follows you using GPS.

Two settings make offline navigation smoother. First, keep your phone's location accuracy on: go to Settings → Location → Location services → Google Location Accuracy and leave it enabled. GPS alone can place you within a few metres outdoors, but the extra help matters in towns with tall buildings. Second, if you know you will be offline, switch the app to WiFi-only mode before you leave (profile picture → Settings → WiFi only) so it stops trying to use mobile data at all.

Searching offline works for places stored in the downloaded map — street names, neighbourhoods, hotels, restaurants and landmarks. What you will not get is the newest places that opened after your download, or reviews and photos, which are fetched online. If a search returns nothing, try a broader term: a street name or neighbourhood instead of a business name.

Step 3: Update, Rename and Delete Downloaded Maps

Downloaded maps go stale. New roads open, one-way systems change, and places close. Google Maps updates downloaded maps automatically when you are on WiFi, but only if you let it — and only within a limited time. Here is how to stay in control:

  • Check expiry: Open profile picture → Offline maps. Each download shows its expiry date. Maps expire after a set period (commonly around a year) and must be refreshed.
  • Update manually: Tap a downloaded map and choose Update. Do this before any important trip, even if the expiry date has not arrived — a map downloaded six months ago may already be missing new roads.
  • Control auto-updates: In the Offline maps settings, you can choose whether updates happen on WiFi only or on any connection, and whether downloads happen automatically. WiFi-only is the safe, free choice.
  • Rename maps: Give each download a clear name — "Lahore trip Nov 2026" beats "Map 3" when you are managing five downloads at 1 AM before a flight.
  • Delete old maps: Tap a download and choose Delete when a trip is over. This frees storage, which matters on phones with limited space.
⚠️ Warning: Never rely on a map you downloaded a year ago for a critical journey. Treat an expired or very old offline map like an old paper map: useful, but verify the route against road signs.

What Offline Maps Cannot Do

Knowing the limits in advance prevents nasty surprises on the road:

  • No live traffic: Offline routes are calculated from road data, not current congestion. A route that is normally 20 minutes might take 40 in rush hour, and the app will not warn you.
  • No automatic rerouting around closures: If a road is closed for construction, an offline map may still route you through it. Watch road signs and be ready to detour.
  • Limited public transport: Bus and train directions need live schedules, so they generally do not work offline. Driving, walking and cycling directions do.
  • No street view, reviews or photos: These are online-only extras. The map itself — roads, place names and basic details — is what you get.
  • Search is limited to the downloaded area: You cannot search for places outside your downloaded rectangles, and results will be sparser than online.

None of these limits is a reason to skip offline maps. They simply mean you should download the map, check the route once while you still have internet, and note any tricky junctions before you set off.

Storage, Battery and Data Tips

  • Check the size first: A dense city can be several hundred megabytes; a rural region of the same area is often much smaller. The app shows the size before you confirm — keep the rectangle tight around where you will actually go.
  • Use an SD card if your phone supports one: In Offline maps settings you can choose SD card storage, which keeps your phone's internal storage free for photos and apps.
  • Download on WiFi, always: Set updates to WiFi-only and do the first download at home. A single city download can otherwise eat a noticeable chunk of a small data plan.
  • Turn the screen off between turns: Voice guidance keeps working with the screen off, and the screen is the biggest battery drain on a long drive. A car charger is still a good idea for trips over an hour.
  • Carry a power bank on walking trips: GPS plus screen-on walking navigation drains a battery in a few hours. Offline maps reduce the drain, but they do not eliminate it.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Downloading too small an area: The classic error is downloading just the city centre and then driving to a hotel outside the rectangle. Zoom out and include the outskirts and the airport.
  • Downloading at the airport on roaming data: That is the most expensive possible moment. Download at home, on WiFi, before you leave.
  • Assuming the download worked: Open the map, switch on aeroplane mode, and try a search and a route before you travel. A two-minute test at home is worth more than any assumption.
  • Forgetting to update: A map from last year may send you down a road that is now one-way in the other direction. Update before every important trip.
  • Relying on one phone: On a road trip, download the same areas on a second phone or a travel companion's phone. Redundancy is free and it has saved many trips.
✅ Result: After this setup, switch on aeroplane mode and navigate to a place in your downloaded area. If voice guidance works and the route renders fully, your offline safety net is ready — and you will never be stranded by a dead signal again.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do offline maps work with no SIM card at all?

Yes. GPS works independently of mobile networks, and a downloaded map needs no data connection. A phone with no SIM can navigate offline exactly like a phone with one, as long as the map is downloaded and location is switched on.

Do offline maps use mobile data in the background?

Not for the map itself. If you leave mobile data on, the app may still try to fetch extras like traffic or place details when a signal appears. WiFi-only mode, or simply keeping data off, keeps the trip fully offline.

How much storage do offline maps need?

It depends on the area and how dense it is. A small town may be tens of megabytes; a large city can be several hundred megabytes. The app shows the exact size before you download, and you can store maps on an SD card if your phone supports one.

Can I download maps for another country?

Yes, for most countries. A small number of regions restrict offline downloads for legal or licensing reasons; if the download option is missing for a particular place, that is why. In that case, download the surrounding areas you can and keep a screenshot of key routes as a backup.

Is there an alternative to Google Maps for offline use?

Yes. Open-source apps such as Organic Maps are built around offline use: you download whole countries at once and navigate without any account. They are a good backup to keep alongside Google Maps, especially for walking and cycling in remote areas.
