How to Remove Bloatware from Your Android Phone Without Rooting (2026 Guide)

Open your app drawer on a brand-new Android phone and you will find apps you never asked for: a second browser, a second gallery, a shopping app, a game, a carrier service, a "cleaner" you did not install. That is bloatware — apps the manufacturer or carrier pre-installed, often with no uninstall button. They take up storage, run in the background, drain battery, show notifications you never wanted, and in some cases collect data you never agreed to share. The good news is that you can remove most of them without rooting your phone, without voiding your warranty, and without any risky software. This guide shows you the safe methods, in order from easiest to most thorough, and tells you exactly which apps you must never touch.

  • Method 1: Uninstall the easy ones normally
  • Method 2: Disable the apps you cannot uninstall

What Bloatware Is, and Why It Matters

Bloatware is any app that came with the phone rather than being chosen by you. It usually falls into three groups. First, manufacturer apps: Samsung, Xiaomi, Oppo and others add their own browser, gallery, app store, cloud service and assistant, duplicating what Google already provides. Second, carrier apps: if you bought the phone through a mobile network, it may include the carrier's account app, a music service, or a sponsored game. Third, third-party deals: some brands are paid to pre-install shopping, social or streaming apps.

One or two extra apps are harmless. The problem is the total: a phone can ship with twenty or more pre-installed apps, many of which run background services, check for updates, and send usage data. On a phone with limited storage, that is real space you paid for. On any phone, it is clutter, notifications and battery drain you did not choose. Removing bloatware is one of the simplest ways to make a phone feel faster — and unlike rooting, every method in this guide is reversible.

Method 1: Uninstall the Easy Ones Normally

Start with the simplest check, because some pre-installed apps can be uninstalled like any other app:

  1. Long-press the app icon on your home screen or in the app drawer.
  2. Tap App info, or tap Uninstall if it appears directly.
  3. If the Uninstall button is active, tap it and confirm. Done — the app is gone.

Alternatively, go to Settings → Apps → See all apps, tap an app, and look for the Uninstall button. Work through your app list once, and remove anything you recognise as unwanted and uninstallable: games you never play, duplicate apps you never open, shopping apps you never use. This pass alone often clears five to ten apps. Anything with a greyed-out Uninstall button needs Method 2 or 3.

Tip: Before removing anything, check how much space and battery it actually uses: Settings → Apps → (app name) shows storage used, and Settings → Battery → Battery usage shows battery use. An app using almost nothing is not worth worrying about — focus on the heavy ones.

Method 2: Disable the Apps You Cannot Uninstall

Most bloatware cannot be uninstalled normally, but almost all of it can be disabled — and for daily use, disabling is nearly as good as uninstalling. A disabled app disappears from your app drawer, cannot run in the background, cannot send notifications, and cannot update itself. Its files stay on the phone, but it is effectively switched off.

  1. Go to Settings → Apps → See all apps.
  2. Tap the app you want to stop.
  3. Tap Disable and confirm. If you see Uninstall updates first, tap that, then Disable.
  4. Repeat for each unwanted app.

Disabling is the safest method in this guide, and it is the one to use if you are unsure about an app: anything disabled can be re-enabled from the same screen, in seconds, with no data loss. It is also the right choice for system apps you do not use but are not sure about — a disabled app cannot cause harm, while a wrongly removed system app can. If your only goal is a clean app drawer and a quieter phone, you can stop here and skip Method 3 entirely.

Method 3: Remove Stubborn Apps with ADB (No Root)

Some apps cannot be disabled either — or you want them fully gone, not just switched off. For those, Android's own developer tool, ADB (Android Debug Bridge), can uninstall an app for your user account without rooting. This is an official Google tool, it does not void your warranty, and it does not unlock your bootloader. It does need a computer and about fifteen minutes of care. Read the warning box below before you start.

  1. On your phone, turn on Developer options: go to Settings → About phone and tap Build number seven times until you see "You are now a developer".
  2. Turn on USB debugging: go to Settings → System → Developer options and switch on USB debugging.
  3. On your computer, install ADB: download the official "SDK Platform-Tools" package from Google's Android developer site, and extract it to a folder. No other download is needed — avoid random "one-click debloat" tools from unknown sites.
  4. Connect the phone to the computer with a USB cable. On the phone, allow USB debugging when asked, and choose "File transfer" mode if prompted.
  5. Open a terminal or command prompt in the platform-tools folder and type adb devices. Your phone should appear in the list. If it says "unauthorized", check the phone screen and accept the debugging prompt again.
  6. List the installed packages with adb shell pm list packages. Find the package name of the app you want to remove (for example, a pre-installed game might be com.example.game — the package name is shown in the list, and it is not the same as the app's display name).
  7. Remove it for your user account with adb shell pm uninstall -k --user 0 com.example.game, replacing the package name with the real one. The -k keeps the app's data, and --user 0 removes it only for your account — the app remains in the system, so a factory reset brings it back.
  8. Repeat step 7 for each app, one at a time, and restart the phone when you are done.
Warning: Remove one app at a time, and only remove apps you have positively identified as bloatware. Never uninstall anything with "system", "provider", "framework", "telephony", "settings" or your manufacturer's core services in the name — removing a system component can break calls, Wi-Fi, the lock screen, or the ability to install apps. If you are not sure what an app is, disable it (Method 2) instead, or search its package name online before removing it. A factory reset restores everything removed this way, but it also erases your own data, so keep a backup.

Which Apps Are Safe to Remove — and Which to Never Touch

As a rule of thumb, apps are safe to remove if they are clearly a standalone product you could have installed yourself from the Play Store: third-party games, shopping apps, streaming apps, duplicate browsers and galleries, social apps you do not use, and carrier promotional apps. These have no role in keeping the phone working.

Never remove: the system launcher (unless you have another launcher installed and set as default), the Settings app, the phone/dialler and messaging apps (unless you have a replacement set as default), the Play Store or its services, anything related to the SIM, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth or the camera, and accessibility services you rely on. Also leave alone anything you do not recognise — an unfamiliar name is a reason to research, not to delete. When in doubt, disable rather than uninstall: it is the same result for your daily use, with a one-tap way back.

What to Do After Removing Bloatware

Restart the phone once you have finished. Then check the results: Settings → Storage should show more free space, Settings → Battery → Battery usage should show less background drain over the next day, and your app drawer should be noticeably shorter. Uninstalled updates for disabled apps can be reclaimed too — if a disabled app still shows large storage, tap Clear cache and Clear storage on its App info page.

Two cautions. First, removing apps for your user account does not free the space they occupy in the system partition, so the storage gain is smaller than the app's full size — the real gains are less background activity, fewer notifications, and a cleaner phone. Second, a major system update can re-enable or restore some pre-installed apps. If an old app reappears after an update, just disable or remove it again — it takes a minute now that you know how.

Common Problems and Fixes

  • No Disable button: Some core apps cannot be disabled. Leave them alone — if the app uses no battery and sends no notifications, it is not hurting you.
  • ADB says "device not found": Check the cable (use a data cable, not a charge-only cable), make sure USB debugging is on, and accept the authorisation prompt on the phone. Try a different USB port.
  • ADB says "Failure [not installed for 0]": The package name is wrong or the app is installed under a different name. Run adb shell pm list packages again and copy the exact name.
  • An app came back after an update: Normal. System updates can restore pre-installed apps. Disable or remove them again.
  • The phone behaves oddly after a removal: If you removed the wrong thing, reinstall it with adb shell cmd package install-existing com.example.app, or re-enable it from Settings if you only disabled it. If the problem is serious, a factory reset restores everything — back up your data first.
✓ Result: Open your app drawer: only apps you chose should be there. Check Settings → Battery tomorrow — background usage should be lower, and no bloatware app should appear in the list at all. If an app you removed is gone from the drawer, cannot run, and cannot notify you, the cleanup worked.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does removing bloatware void my warranty?

No. Uninstalling, disabling, and the ADB method in this guide do not root the phone, do not unlock the bootloader, and do not void the warranty. Rooting is a different, deeper change — and this guide never needs it.

Is it safe to use ADB?

Yes, when you use the official platform-tools from Google and only run the uninstall command on apps you have identified. ADB is a developer tool built by Google for exactly this kind of work. The risk is not the tool, it is removing the wrong package — which is why the warning in this guide matters.

Will a factory reset bring bloatware back?

Yes. Apps removed with ADB for your user account are still stored in the system, so a factory reset restores them. That is also what makes the method safe: nothing is permanently destroyed.

Why can I not uninstall some apps at all?

Because the manufacturer or carrier placed them in the system partition, where normal uninstall is blocked. Disabling switches them off, and ADB removes them for your user account — both are legitimate ways around that block without rooting.

Should I use a "debloat" app from the Play Store?

Be careful. Some are genuine helpers, but many ask for broad permissions or show ads, and a few are outright scams. The manual methods in this guide — Settings and official ADB — need no third-party app at all, and that is the safest route.

Most phones carry more pre-installed apps than their owners ever use, and every one of them is a small tax on storage, battery and attention. Start with the easy uninstalls, disable what you cannot uninstall, and use ADB only for the stubborn remainder — one app at a time, never guessing. Your phone will feel lighter, quieter and more yours, and you will have done it without rooting, without risking your warranty, and with a way back for every single change.
