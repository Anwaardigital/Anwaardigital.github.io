How to Use Android Accessibility Features: Make Your Phone Easier to See, Hear and Use (2026 Guide)

Every Android phone ships with a powerful set of accessibility features — tools that make text bigger, read the screen aloud, caption every video, and even let you control the whole phone with your voice. They are free, they are already installed, and they are not only for people with a disability: bigger text helps tired eyes, captions help in a noisy room, and voice control helps when your hands are full. Yet most people never open the Accessibility menu at all. This guide walks you through the most useful features, one by one, with the exact steps to turn each one on — and shows how to set up a phone for an elderly parent or anyone who finds small text and tiny buttons hard to use.

Where to find Android accessibility settings

Almost every accessibility feature lives in one place: Settings → Accessibility. On Samsung phones the path is the same, and on Pixel and most other Android phones it is also Settings → Accessibility. If you cannot find it, open Settings and type "Accessibility" in the search bar at the top — it will take you straight there.

Inside, the features are grouped by what they help with: Vision, Hearing, Interaction, and sometimes General. You do not need to turn everything on at once. The best approach is to switch on one feature, try it for a day, and then add another if you need it. Every feature can be switched off again just as easily, so there is no risk in experimenting.

Tip: Many accessibility features can be assigned to a shortcut — a long-press of both volume buttons, or a floating button on screen — so you can turn them on and off in seconds. We cover shortcuts at the end of this guide.

Making text and the screen easier to see

Small text is the most common complaint about smartphones, and it is also the easiest to fix.

Font size

Go to Settings → Accessibility → Font size (on some phones it is under Settings → Display → Font size). Drag the slider to the right and the text in menus, messages and most apps grows instantly. The largest setting is roughly twice the default size. This affects system text and most apps that respect the system font size, including WhatsApp, Chrome and Gmail.

Display size

Font size only changes text. Display size (Settings → Accessibility → Display size, or Settings → Display → Display size) makes everything bigger — icons, buttons, menus and text together. If you find yourself tapping the wrong button because targets are too small, raise the display size one or two steps instead of only the font size. Together, a larger font and a larger display size transform a phone that felt cramped into one that feels comfortable.

Bold text and high contrast

On the same Accessibility screen you will find Bold text, which thickens all system text and makes it easier to read at a glance. High contrast text (on Samsung, under Visibility enhancements) adds an outline to text so it stands out against busy backgrounds. Both are simple on/off switches with an immediate effect.

Dark theme

Dark theme (Settings → Display → Dark theme) turns bright white screens dark, which many people find easier on the eyes, especially at night, and it also saves battery on phones with OLED screens. Android can switch it on automatically at sunset if you prefer. For more on saving battery while using your phone, see our guide to extending Android battery life.

Magnification and colour adjustments

Magnification

Magnification (Settings → Accessibility → Magnification) lets you zoom into any part of the screen, even in apps that do not support pinch-to-zoom. Once it is on, triple-tap the screen to zoom in, drag two fingers to move around, and triple-tap again to zoom out. You can also choose the "Magnify with shortcut" option, which shows a small button at the edge of the screen — tap it, then tap the area you want to enlarge. It is ideal for reading a small label, a medicine leaflet, or a tiny line of text in a photo.

Colour correction and colour inversion

Colour correction adjusts colours for common types of colour blindness (red-green, blue-yellow). If colours look confusing or washed out, try the different modes until one looks right. Colour inversion flips all colours on the screen — useful for some people with light sensitivity, though it also inverts photos, so most people prefer dark theme instead.

Remove animations

If moving animations make you feel dizzy, turn on Remove animations (Settings → Accessibility → Remove animations). It stops the sliding and zooming transitions between screens, which also makes the phone feel a little faster. If your phone is generally slow as well, our guide on speeding up a slow Android phone covers the bigger fixes.

TalkBack: your phone reads the screen aloud

TalkBack is Android's built-in screen reader. When it is on, your phone speaks everything you touch — buttons, menus, messages and notifications — so you can use the phone without looking at the screen at all. It is designed for people with low vision, but it is also useful to try once so you understand how it works if you ever need to help someone else.

How to turn TalkBack on

  1. Go to Settings → Accessibility → TalkBack and switch it on. Android will show a short tutorial — it is worth reading, because TalkBack changes how touch works.
  2. Or use the shortcut: press and hold both volume buttons for three seconds. The first time you do this, Android will ask which feature the shortcut should control — choose TalkBack.

How TalkBack gestures work

With TalkBack on, a single tap only selects an item and reads its name aloud. To actually open it, double-tap. To scroll, drag with two fingers instead of one. This takes a few minutes to get used to, and it is the reason TalkBack should only be switched on when it is genuinely needed — otherwise normal use becomes frustrating. To switch it off, go back to Settings → Accessibility → TalkBack (remember: tap once to select, double-tap to open) or press both volume buttons for three seconds again.

Select to Speak

If TalkBack feels like too much, try Select to Speak instead (Settings → Accessibility → Select to Speak). It adds a small button to your screen; tap it, then tap any text, and your phone reads just that text aloud. It is perfect for reading a long article or a message when your eyes are tired, without changing how the rest of the phone works.

Warning: Never turn TalkBack on as a joke on someone else's phone. It changes all touch gestures, and a person who does not know the gestures can be locked out of their own phone until someone turns it off for them.

Live Caption and hearing support

Live Caption

Live Caption (Settings → Accessibility → Live Caption) automatically adds captions to any video or audio playing on your phone — YouTube, WhatsApp voice notes, video calls, even videos in your gallery. The captions appear in a small box you can drag anywhere on screen, and the processing happens entirely on your phone, so it works without an internet connection and nothing is sent to Google. It is a genuine help for anyone with hearing loss, and it is also handy when you want to watch a video with the sound off.

Sound Amplifier

Sound Amplifier (Settings → Accessibility → Sound Amplifier) turns your phone into a hearing aid of sorts: with wired or Bluetooth headphones, it amplifies quiet sounds around you and filters out background noise. It can help in a conversation in a noisy room or when watching TV at low volume.

Hearing aid support and mono audio

Android supports Bluetooth hearing aids directly — pair them like any Bluetooth device and the sound goes straight to the hearing aid. If you hear better in one ear than the other, turn on Mono audio (Settings → Accessibility → Mono audio) so both channels play in both ears instead of splitting left and right, and use the Audio balance slider to favour your stronger ear. For safer listening in general, see our guide to fixing low volume and cleaning Android speakers safely.

Flash notifications

If you often miss the sound of a notification, turn on Flash notification (Settings → Accessibility → Flash notification, or on Samsung under Advanced settings). Your camera flash blinks when a call or message arrives — a silent, visual alert that is hard to miss.

Voice Access and Switch Access: control without touching

Voice Access

Voice Access (Settings → Accessibility → Voice Access) lets you control the entire phone with your voice. Once it is on, every button and item on screen gets a small number next to it — say the number to tap it, or say commands like "go back", "scroll down", "open WhatsApp", or "type hello". It is designed for people who cannot easily touch the screen, but it is also useful when your hands are wet, dirty, or busy cooking. You will need to download the Voice Access app from the Play Store the first time you switch it on.

Switch Access

Switch Access is for people who use one or two external switches (or even the volume buttons) instead of the touchscreen. It scans through the items on screen one by one, and you press your switch to select the highlighted item. Setting it up takes a little patience, but for someone who cannot use a touchscreen at all, it makes the phone usable again.

Interaction controls

Two smaller settings are worth knowing: Touch & hold delay lets you change how long you must hold your finger down before a long-press registers — increase it if you often trigger long-presses by accident. Time to take action gives you more seconds to respond to pop-ups and temporary messages before they disappear, which is helpful if you read slowly or your hands are unsteady.

Setting up a phone for elderly parents

If you are setting up a phone for a parent or grandparent, a few accessibility changes together make a big difference:

  1. Increase font size and display size two steps each — this single change helps more than anything else.
  2. Turn on bold text and switch the phone to dark theme if they prefer it.
  3. Set up the accessibility shortcut (below) so they can magnify the screen whenever they need it.
  4. Turn on Live Caption if their hearing is weak, and enable flash notifications so they do not miss calls.
  5. Simplify the home screen: remove extra widgets, keep only the apps they use, and make the icons as large as the display size allows. Our guide to organising the Android home screen shows how to do this cleanly.
  6. Set up emergency SOS so they can call for help quickly — see our emergency SOS setup guide.

If you are setting up a phone for an elderly parent from scratch, our guide to setting up Android for elderly parents covers the whole process, and this accessibility guide fills in the detail. Sit with them for ten minutes, show them the magnification gesture and the volume-button shortcut, and write the steps on a card they can keep by the phone.

Quick-access shortcuts and troubleshooting

Accessibility shortcuts

Opening Settings every time is slow, so Android offers two quick ways to reach your favourite accessibility features:

  • Volume key shortcut: go to Settings → Accessibility → (the feature) → Shortcut, and turn on "Use volume key shortcut". Now pressing both volume buttons for three seconds toggles that feature on and off.
  • Accessibility button: in Settings → Accessibility → Accessibility button, you can add a small floating button to the edge of your screen. Tap it to open a menu of your chosen accessibility features. On Samsung phones this is called the "Accessibility button" under Advanced settings.

Common problems

  • A feature is missing from Settings: the exact list depends on your Android version and phone brand. Update your phone (Settings → Software update) and check again — newer Android versions add features. Samsung phones put many of these under Settings → Accessibility → Visibility enhancements or Hearing enhancements.
  • Text is huge in one app but normal elsewhere: some apps have their own font size setting that overrides the system one. Check the app's own settings (in WhatsApp, for example: Settings → Chats → Font size).
  • TalkBack turned on by accident and you cannot use the phone: press and hold both volume buttons for three seconds to toggle it off. If that shortcut was never set up, ask someone to go to Settings → Accessibility → TalkBack and switch it off — remembering that with TalkBack on, you tap once to select and double-tap to open.
  • Live Caption is not appearing: it needs Android 10 or newer, and it only captions media playing through the phone's speakers or headphones. If it is on but silent, check that the media volume is up and that the app is actually playing sound.
  • Magnification zooms when you do not want it: you are probably triple-tapping by accident. Turn magnification off, or switch to the shortcut-button mode instead of triple-tap, in Settings → Accessibility → Magnification.

Frequently asked questions

Are Android accessibility features free?

Yes. Every feature in this guide is built into Android and costs nothing. The only exception is Voice Access, which is a free download from the Play Store. Be careful of third-party "accessibility" apps that charge a subscription — the built-in tools do the same job for free.

Do accessibility features slow down my phone?

For most features — font size, dark theme, magnification, Live Caption — the effect is negligible. TalkBack and Voice Access use more processing power because they are constantly listening and speaking, so you may notice slightly faster battery use while they are active, but they do not damage the phone.

Can I use accessibility features on a child's phone?

Yes, and some are genuinely useful for children — larger text for early readers, or "Time to take action" for a child who reads slowly. For controlling what a child can access, though, you want parental controls rather than accessibility settings. Our Android parental controls guide covers that properly.

Will these settings survive a phone reset or a new phone?

If you back up your phone to your Google account, most accessibility settings are restored when you set up a new phone. If you are moving to a new phone, our guide to transferring data to a new Android phone walks through the whole move. After a factory reset, you will need to turn the features on again.

Do I need to install any apps for these features?

No, except Voice Access, which Android will offer to install from the Play Store when you first switch it on. Everything else is already on your phone — you only need to know where the switch is.

Accessibility features are some of the best work Google and the phone makers have ever done, and they sit quietly in a menu most people never open. Whether your eyes are tired, your hearing is weaker than it was, your hands are busy, or you are setting up a phone for someone you love, ten minutes in Settings → Accessibility can make the difference between a phone that fights you and a phone that fits you. Turn on one feature today — bigger text is the easiest place to start — and see how much easier your phone feels.
