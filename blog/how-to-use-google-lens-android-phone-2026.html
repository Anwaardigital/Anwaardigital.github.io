How to Use Google Lens on Android Phone: Scan, Translate and Identify Anything (2026 Guide)

You are standing in front of a plant you cannot name, a shop sign written in a language you cannot read, or a product you want to buy but do not know the price of. Normally you would type a description into Google and hope for the best. Google Lens skips the typing: you point your camera at the thing itself, and your phone tells you what it is, translates it, copies the text off it, or finds where to buy it. It is free, it is already on most Android phones, and it works on photos you have already taken as well as on live camera view. This guide shows you every way to open Lens, what each of its modes does, and how to use it safely.

What Google Lens is and what it can do

Google Lens is a visual search tool built by Google. Instead of searching with words, you search with a picture. Lens looks at what your camera sees, recognises the objects, text and landmarks in the image, and matches them against Google's search index. The result is an answer in seconds — the name of a flower, the translation of a menu, the price of a pair of shoes, or the history of a building.

Lens is not a separate app you have to buy. On most Android phones it is built into the Google app, the Camera app, and Google Photos. It needs an internet connection for most results, because the recognition happens on Google's servers, but the camera and the scanning itself work offline. Lens is free, has no ads inside the tool itself, and does not need a separate account beyond the Google account already on your phone.

Tip: If you have used Lens before and it looked different, that is normal — Google updates its layout regularly. The modes described here (Search, Translate, Text, Homework, Shopping) are the ones you will find in the current version, though their exact order may vary slightly by phone.

How to open Google Lens — four ways

There is no single "right" way to open Lens. Use whichever is fastest in the moment:

1. From the Google app

Open the Google app, then tap the small camera icon (the Lens icon) in the search bar at the top. This is the most reliable way, and it works on almost every Android phone.

2. From your Camera app

On Pixel phones and many Samsung and other Android phones, the Camera app has a Lens option in its modes list, or a small Lens icon on the viewfinder. Open the camera, switch to the Lens mode, and point at what you want to identify. On Samsung phones, you may find Lens inside the camera's "More" menu.

3. From Google Photos

Open any photo in Google Photos and tap the Lens icon at the bottom. This is the best option when the thing you want to identify is already in your gallery — a photo you took on holiday, a screenshot of a product, or a picture a friend sent you. You can also use this on screenshots, which is handy for translating text in an image someone shared with you.

4. From Google Assistant or your home screen

On some phones you can say "Hey Google, open Lens", or long-press the Google app icon and tap Lens from the shortcuts. You can also add the Lens widget to your home screen: long-press an empty area, tap Widgets, find the Google widgets, and drag the Lens widget onto your home screen for one-tap access.

The first time you open Lens, it will ask for permission to use your camera. Tap "Allow" — without it, Lens cannot see anything. It may also ask to turn on location for better results with landmarks; you can decline that and still use everything else.

Searching with a photo

This is the core of Lens. Point your camera at something, tap the shutter button (or tap the object on screen), and Lens shows visually similar results with links to websites, shops and information pages.

  1. Open Lens using any method above.
  2. Point your camera at the object so it fills a good part of the frame. Hold the phone steady and make sure there is enough light.
  3. Tap the shutter button to take a Lens snapshot, or tap directly on the object in the viewfinder.
  4. Browse the results. Lens shows matching images and web pages. Scroll down for more, and tap any result to open it.

Visual search works best on clear, well-lit subjects with a plain background. If Lens gives wrong or vague results, move closer, change the angle, or crop the photo so only the object is in the frame. For more on taking clear phone photos, see our guide to taking better photos on Android.

Translating text with your camera

Lens's Translate mode is one of its most useful features, especially when travelling or reading a document in another language. It can translate printed text in real time — the translation appears on your screen over the original words.

  1. Open Lens and tap Translate at the bottom of the screen.
  2. Choose the languages: tap the language at the top left (the language of the text) and the top right (the language you want). "Auto detect" usually works for the source language.
  3. Point your camera at the text — a sign, a menu, a book page, a label. The translation appears live on screen.
  4. Tap the shutter button to freeze the translation so you can read it carefully, copy it, or listen to it read aloud.

Translate works on printed text, handwriting (if it is neat), and text in photos. It is not perfect — idioms, slang and very stylised fonts can confuse it — so for anything important, like medicine instructions or a contract, double-check with a human translator. But for menus, street signs, product labels and everyday reading, it is remarkably good. If you regularly work with documents on your phone, our guide to scanning documents on Android pairs well with this feature.

Tip: You can translate text in a photo you already have. Open the photo in Google Photos, tap the Lens icon, then tap Translate. This is useful for translating screenshots of messages or posts sent to you in another language.

Copying and scanning printed text

The Text mode turns printed words into digital text you can copy, paste and search. It is a lifesaver for long passages from books, business cards, receipts and handwritten notes.

  1. Open Lens and tap Text at the bottom.
  2. Point at the text and tap the shutter button. Lens highlights all the text it found.
  3. Select what you need by dragging over it, just like selecting text on a web page.
  4. Tap "Copy text" to put it on your clipboard, or tap "Search" to Google the selected words, or tap "Listen" to hear it read aloud.

This is the fastest way to digitise a paper document: point, scan, copy, paste into your notes app. For turning whole pages into clean PDFs, see our document scanning guide. One caution: proofread the copied text before you send it anywhere important — Lens can misread similar-looking letters, especially in small or faded print.

Identifying plants, animals and products

Lens is surprisingly good at naming the natural world. Point it at a flower in a garden, a bird in a park, or a dog at a friend's house, and it will usually tell you the species or breed, with links to learn more. This works the same way as photo search — open Lens, point, tap — but here are a few tips for better results:

  • Plants: photograph the flower or leaf close up, in daylight. A photo of the whole plant from far away is harder to identify than one clear flower.
  • Animals: get the animal's face and body in frame. For pets, a clear side photo works better than a blurry running shot.
  • Products: Lens recognises many branded products — shoes, bags, furniture, electronics. Tap the product and Lens shows similar items and where to buy them.
  • Landmarks and buildings: point at a famous building or monument and Lens will usually name it and offer history, opening hours and reviews.
  • Food: point at a dish and Lens can often identify it and show recipes.

Remember that Lens gives its best guess based on matching images. For plants and mushrooms, never eat anything based on a Lens result alone — some lookalikes are poisonous, and only an expert identification is safe.

Shopping and homework help

Shopping with Lens

Tap the Shopping tab in Lens and point at a product — or at a photo of one — and Lens shows matching products from online stores, with prices where available. It is useful when you see something in a shop or on a friend and want to compare prices before buying. You can also use it on clothes: point at an outfit and Lens finds similar items. Always compare prices in more than one place before buying, and check the seller's reputation — a low price from an unknown shop is a warning sign. Our guide to spotting fake apps covers the same habit of checking before you trust, and it applies to shopping sites too.

Homework help

The Homework tab lets students point the camera at a maths problem or a question and get a step-by-step explanation. It is genuinely helpful for understanding how an answer is reached — but it should be used to learn, not to copy answers without understanding them. Privacy tips

Lens sends the images you scan to Google's servers to analyse them, so a few habits keep your privacy in good shape:

  • Do not scan sensitive documents. Avoid pointing Lens at identity cards, bank cards, passwords, medical records or private letters. Use your phone's built-in scanner or type the details manually instead.
  • Check your Lens history. Lens searches are saved in your Google account activity. You can review and delete them at myactivity.google.com, under the Lens filter.
  • Turn off location if you do not need it. Lens works fine without location permission; it only helps with landmark results.
  • Be careful with other people's photos. Scanning a photo of someone else without their knowledge can be a privacy issue — use Lens on your own photos and things in public, not on private images of other people.
Warning: Lens results come from the open web, and the web is not always right. Do not make health, safety or money decisions based on a Lens result alone — especially plant and mushroom identification, medical labels, or product authenticity. Verify important information from a trusted source.

Troubleshooting: when Lens does not work

  • Lens is missing from the Google app: update the Google app from the Play Store. On very old phones, Lens may not be supported at all — it needs Android 6 or newer and a recent Google app version.
  • Results are wrong or vague: improve the light, move closer, and make sure the object fills the frame. Tap on the object itself so Lens knows what to focus on.
  • Translate shows the wrong language: tap the language selector at the top and set the source language manually instead of "Auto detect".
  • Lens says "no results" or loads forever: check your internet connection — Lens needs to be online for most results. If the connection is fine, close and reopen the Google app.
  • Text mode cannot read the text: hold the phone steady, use better light, and keep the page flat. Curved book pages and glossy surfaces reflect light and confuse the scanner.
  • Camera permission was denied: go to Settings → Apps → Google → Permissions → Camera, and set it to "Allow only while using the app".

Frequently asked questions

Is Google Lens free?

Yes. Lens is completely free on Android, with no subscription and no in-app purchases. It comes with the Google app, which is preinstalled on most Android phones.

Does Google Lens work without the internet?

Mostly no — the recognition and translation need an internet connection. The camera itself works offline, and some basic text detection may work on-device, but for real results you need to be online.

Can I use Google Lens on photos already on my phone?

Yes. Open any photo in Google Photos and tap the Lens icon, or open Lens and tap the gallery icon to choose an image from your phone.

Does Lens work on iPhone?

Yes, Lens is available inside the Google app and Google Photos on iPhone too, but this guide covers the Android experience, where it is most deeply integrated.

Is my photo stored by Google when I use Lens?

Images you scan are sent to Google to produce results and may be saved in your Google account activity. You can review and delete this history at myactivity.google.com, and you can turn off activity saving in your Google account settings.

Google Lens is one of those tools that feels like a small piece of the future hiding in your pocket: a camera that can read, translate, identify and shop. It costs nothing, it is already on your phone, and once you build the habit of opening it instead of typing a description, you will wonder how you managed without it. Try it today on the first thing you cannot name — a plant, a sign, a gadget — and see what your camera already knows.
